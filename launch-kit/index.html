Launch Kit A shared start for teams of students, partner institutions, and the communities we design with.

The strongest community design happens when everyone builds on each other's strengths from day one. This kit walks your team — NCCU students, collaborators from partner schools and institutions, and the community partners and clients you're designing with — through a shared start: understanding how each of you communicates and thinks, then setting goals, roles, ground rules, and a timeline together. Work through it before the design work begins, and you'll move faster, communicate better, and build the trust good community design depends on.

Individual Activities 30 MIN

To start, explore how you communicate and think on your own. Find a quiet moment to take the two assessments, then gather a few details about yourself to share with your team in the group activities.

1

Communication Style 10 min

How you listen, speak, and share work shapes every collaboration — and mixed teams of students, faculty, and community partners bring very different habits to the table. This short self-assessment names your communication style so your team can recognize and work with each other's differences instead of talking past them.

Steps
  • Open the Communication Style Finder and take it (about 5 minutes).
  • Note your style and the "how to work with me" tips it gives you.
  • Bring your result to Activity 3 and share it with the team in Activity 5.
Take the Communication Style Finder
2

Thinking Style 10 min

People don't just communicate differently — they think differently. One teammate wants the big picture, another wants the facts, another wants to try something and see what works. This assessment maps how you approach ideas and problems, so your team can turn those different lenses into a strength instead of a source of friction.

Steps
  • Open the Thinking Style Finder and rank your way through it (about 10 minutes).
  • Note your top style(s) and what they say about how you work.
  • Bring your result to Activity 3 and share it in Activity 5.

Team Activities 2.5 HRS

Now that everyone has gathered their insights and knows a little about how they communicate and think, it's time to collaborate. Work through the rest together — on a whiteboard, a shared doc, or right here — and keep a master copy for your team.

4

Goals & Deliverables 20 min

Every project carries its own challenges. Your team's ability to pool strengths and solve problems creatively is what moves it forward. Agree on one clear goal and the deliverables that will get you there — and make sure the community partner's priorities are in the goal, not just the team's.

Example · Briggs Community Garden (with Duke)
Goal:
Design a welcoming, low-maintenance community garden at Briggs that neighbors of all ages can plant, gather, and learn in — shaped by what residents say they want.
Deliverables:
  • Site research & community input summary
  • Concept plan & planting design
  • Materials / plant list & budget
  • Final presentation to partners
  • Process book

Your goal & deliverables

  • In one or two sentences, state your project goal.
  • List what your team will make or hand off.
5

Team Insights 20 min

Everyone brings distinct backgrounds, knowledge, and skills — and on a cross-institution team, different calendars and obligations too. Sharing your insight cards and your availability is the first step to a project you can actually manage together.

Steps
  • Read your Activity 3 insight cards together and get to know each other.
  • Share your communication and thinking styles — and one way each of you likes to be worked with.
  • Compare availability grids to find common meeting times.
  • Collect everyone's contact info in one shared place.
6

Roles & Responsibilities 20 min

Clear roles keep a project moving and make sure the community partner's voice has a seat at the table. Decide which roles your project needs, what each one takes, and who's best suited — drawing on the strengths you just shared.

Example roles for a community design project
Project Coordinator — keeps the schedule, agendas, and hand-offs on track. Takes organization and follow-through.
Community Liaison — keeps residents' and the client's voice central; sets up input sessions. Takes listening and local trust.
Research & Site Lead — gathers site, precedent, and community data. Takes curiosity and rigor.
Design & Production Lead — drives drawings, models, and the look of deliverables. Takes design chops and craft.
Documentation & Comms — captures the process and handles outside communication. Takes clear writing and tidy files.

Your roles

  • Which roles does your project need? What does each one take?
  • Who's the best fit — and does the partner/client have a role too?
7

Ground Rules 20 min

Ground rules set shared expectations — especially when teammates come from different schools, cultures, and working norms. Agree on how you'll communicate, share files, make decisions, and hold each other accountable.

Example
Communication: Group text for quick things, email for official, shared channel for the rest.
File management: One shared Drive/Dropbox folder, named files, single source of truth.
Meetings & prep: Weekly check-in, agenda ahead of time, come prepared.
Decision making: Consensus where we can; the lead decides if we're stuck; the community partner has the final say on community priorities.
Accountability: If you can't deliver, tell the team early so we can adjust.

Your ground rules

  • How will your team communicate, and share files and resources?
  • How will you make decisions — and where does the client's voice lead?
  • What are your attendance and prep expectations, and how will you handle it when someone falls behind?
8

Project Road Map 40 min

Mapping your phases gives the whole team — and your partners — a shared picture of the work over time. Lay out your deliverables from Activity 4 across the weeks you have, and build in time for community input and review. Adapt it as you go.

Your road map

  • List your deliverables from Activity 4 and plot each one's start and due dates.
  • Build in checkpoints for community input and partner review.

Pick someone to collect and host your team's outcomes — insight cards, goal and deliverables, roles, ground rules, and road map — in the shared workspace you chose in Activity 7. [ About the Eyrie Initiative ]

The Eyrie Initiative Community Design Center at North Carolina Central University is a student-driven design practice that partners with neighborhoods, institutions, and organizations on real community design projects — from public spaces and gardens to maps, graphics, and the built environment. Students work alongside community members and collaborators from partner schools to turn local priorities into design that gets built and used.

Frameworks behind this kit. The two self-assessments draw on widely used models of collaboration: a four-part communication-styles framework (commonly called DISC) and a five-part model of thinking styles. Both are original instruments written for the Eyrie Initiative, not copies of any proprietary assessment.
